A 2023 Pew Research Center survey found that 47% of U.S. adults under 50 who do not have children cited "the state of the world" as a reason they don't expect to have any. It is not hard to understand why. Children today face things their parents never contended with, and in the age of technology, the weight of that reality can feel crushing. Even some parents who have children sometimes wonder, quietly, whether they made the right choice.

These concerns are real, but they do not tell the whole story. Children are a blessing from the Lord, and it is His blessing that makes rich, without sorrow added to it (Proverbs 10:22). Watching a child grow at different stages of life is one of the most precious experiences a parent can have. And yet the question remains: how do we raise children well in a world that keeps getting more uncertain, confusing, and chaotic?

A few years ago, our daughter, who was seven at the time and in second grade, came home and told us that a boy had asked her to be his girlfriend. He had even given her money and told her not to tell anyone. When he found out she had come home and told us, he was upset. As a father, my first instinct was to find the boy and give him a little more than a fierce warning that he must never come near my daughter again. My wife and I have been raising our children diligently and intentionally, praying regularly for them that they would not be swept up in the corruption of this generation. The thought of a boy approaching our daughter at that age was simply unimaginable to us. But that moment sharpened our awareness of the pressures children face today. The truth is that it is not enough to plant good seeds in your children; you must also nurture and protect them from the weeds that seek to destroy.

Our desire and prayer as we raise our children is that they will be children with clear vision rooted in the ways of the Lord. That vision offers direction without becoming a rigid script that leaves no room for who they are as individuals. Whatever your beliefs or background, one important question every parent must ask themselves is this: how do we prepare children for a world we cannot predict?

## Give them a compass, not a fixed itinerary

We cannot know every challenge our children will face. We can, however, help them develop values that will guide how they respond. One of the most helpful things I have found is having a clear family vision, something simple and livable.

One might sound like this: *In our family, we tell the truth, treat people with respect, take responsibility, and help others.* The important thing is not just the words; it is what those words look like in everyday moments. What does honesty mean when admitting a mistake might bring consequences? What does kindness require when someone is being left out?

Proverbs 22:6 calls us to intentional guidance in how we raise our children. It does not promise a trouble-free future, but it is a call to take this responsibility seriously. The goal is not to choose every destination for our children. It is to help them carry a trustworthy compass.

## Make home a place where struggle can be spoken

One thing the Army has taught me is that resilience is not the absence of sadness, fear, or disappointment. It is not simply toughness; it is an internal quality that is learned, developed, and strengthened through training and the right environment. Children need to know that experiencing difficulty does not make them weak.

Harvard University's Center on the Developing Child identifies stable, supportive relationships with adults as a central protective factor for children facing adversity. That is a helpful reminder: as parents, our presence matters, not just our advice. Sometimes a child does not need a lecture. They just need a father who shows up, or a mother who truly sees them and loves them unconditionally. When a child says, *"I cannot do this,"* we can resist the urge to immediately reply, *"Of course you can."* We might begin instead with, *"That sounds hard. Tell me what happened."*

Listening does not mean removing boundaries or agreeing with everything. It means understanding before correcting. My wife and I are raising our children to know they can bring difficult questions and honest mistakes to us, not only their achievements. A home where children can speak freely gives parents the opportunity to guide them exactly when guidance is needed most.

## Teach them what to do with strong feelings

*"Be strong"* is incomplete advice if we never show what strength actually looks like. Proverbs 29:11 says that "A fool vents all his feelings, but a wise man holds them back." Modeling composure shows children how wisdom brings calm to a heated moment.

Sometimes strength means pausing before reacting. Sometimes it means naming a feeling out loud and asking for help. Children learn how to navigate their inner world by watching how the adults around them honestly process life. When a parent handles a strong emotion with composure in front of their children, it gives those children a picture of what self-control looks like in real life, not just a rule to follow. *"I am frustrated right now, so I am going to pause before we continue this conversation."* That one sentence teaches more than a lecture on anger management ever could. And when we respond badly, which we will, an honest apology shows children how to take responsibility and repair a relationship.

For our family, prayer belongs naturally here. We can bring our fears to God and take practical action at the same time. Prayer and seeking help are not competing choices; they work together.

## Give them purpose without making love conditional

I want my daughters to have vision. But I do not want them to confuse their worth with their performance.

As parents, we can celebrate effort, honesty, kindness, and the willingness to learn, not just grades, prizes, or recognition. After a disappointment, a useful conversation might begin with: *"What did you learn? What would you do differently? How can I support you in trying again?"*

Purpose can also grow through contribution. In our home, all of our children take turns leading family devotions on Saturday mornings. They prepare slides, present the content, and teach. It develops them spiritually and builds their confidence and communication skills. Children do not have to wait until adulthood to make a meaningful difference.

## Prepare them with hope

I cannot promise my daughters an easy future. What I can give them is clear values, dependable love, opportunities to practice courage, and the confidence that asking God for help is the solution to all problems.

Raising resilient children is not about making them hard enough to feel nothing. It is about helping them face what they feel, respond wisely, and remain connected to the people around them.

We do not need to have every answer. We just need to keep showing up with guidance, with humility, and with hope, trusting in the promises of God.
