I'll be honest with you: I don't have this figured out. I'm writing this as someone who is challenged every day to maximize all 24 hours of my day. Sometimes these challenges have been tested, as a U.S. Senate page, tutoring students at Kumon, and keeping up with a full course load at one of the top-ranked high schools in the state; "balance" is something I'm still chasing, not something I've caught.

Last year, I got a front-row seat to what "too much" actually looks like. Serving as a Senate page meant waking up at 5 a.m. daily and working 60+ hours a week, while still keeping up with academics and honestly competing with 29 other students. It was one of the hardest stretches of my life, and it taught me something I didn't expect: that kind of grind used to be a college-student problem. It isn't anymore. I watched it happen to high schoolers around me too — kids juggling part-time jobs, community college dual-enrollment classes, sports, and family responsibilities, all before they've even graduated. If that's you, you're not imagining it. The load really has gotten heavier for our generation.

So this isn't a "10 tips from an expert" article. It's what I've actually seen work for me and for the driven, overloaded people I've been surrounded by.

## Build Independent Learning Skills (No One Will Do It For You)

Nobody is going to walk you through every assignment, every deadline, or every gap in your schedule — not in high school, and definitely not after. Tutoring younger students at Kumon actually drove this home for me: the kids who improved fastest weren't the ones who got the most hand-holding; they were the ones who learned to catch their own mistakes. The same is true for us. The students I've watched handle heavy loads well are the ones who stopped waiting for someone to explain things and started figuring out how to teach themselves — how to read a syllabus and reverse-engineer a plan, how to ask better questions when they're stuck, how to notice what they don't understand before it becomes a crisis. This is a skill, not a personality trait. You build it by doing it badly a few times first.

## Change Your Study Environment to Break the Distraction Loop

One thing that's worked consistently for me: when I need to actually focus, I don't study at home. I go somewhere further away, somewhere less comfortable — a library, a study room, anywhere that isn't my bed or my couch. Home is full of tiny permissions to be distracted. Removing yourself from that environment removes the negotiation with yourself. If you're serious about a study block or a work session, physically leave the place where "just five more minutes of scrolling" feels normal.

## Protect Your Sleep — It's Not Negotiable

\
I want to be straightforward here: during my time as a Senate page, sleep was the first thing to go, and it cost me. I don't say that to sound impressive — I say it because it wasn't sustainable, and I don't think it's supposed to be. Whatever else you cut from your schedule, going to bed at a real time has to be close to the last thing you sacrifice. You cannot out-hustle a sleep deficit. Your brain, your mood, and honestly your ability to keep doing everything else you're trying to do all run on it.
